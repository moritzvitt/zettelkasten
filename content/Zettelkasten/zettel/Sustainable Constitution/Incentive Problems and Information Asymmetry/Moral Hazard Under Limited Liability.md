## Claim

Moral hazard under limited liability arises when owners retain a company's upside while part of the extreme downside risk falls on creditors, victims, employees, or the public.

Limited liability is not inherently wrong. It enables investment because owners are not liable with all their personal assets for every corporate debt. It becomes problematic when long-term risks are accumulated, profits are distributed beforehand, and later damages can be paid only from limited corporate assets.

Examples that reveal this pattern include Purdue Pharma and the Sackler family, asbestos companies, and Bhopal. Not all cases work in the same way: Deepwater Horizon shows instead that even high corporate penalties do not automatically establish personal liability or fully internalize social harm.

The political question is therefore not simply whether limited liability should be abolished. It is: Which institutions prevent ownership from permanently privatizing profits while socializing systemic risks?

## Why It Matters

This note marks an institutional point at which a sustainable constitution would need to organize responsibility, ownership, power, or externalities more clearly.
